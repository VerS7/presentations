# Story Structure & Narrative Arcs

A presentation without a story is an unstructured data dump. A story establishes emotional stakes, builds curiosity, and guides the audience toward a clear conclusion.

---

## 1. The Core Spine (Kenn Adams / Pixar Formula)

Use this spine during brainstorming to establish the dramatic tension:

```text
Once upon a time [The baseline situation and context]
Every day [The routine, friction, and daily compromises]
One day [The catalyst: a market change, breakthrough, or crisis]
Because of that [The initial impact and compounding problem]
Because of that [The realization that old methods are broken]
Until finally [The new way forward, creating permanent leverage]
```

### Example (Enterprise Platform Deck):
- **Once upon a time:** Teams built software using fragmented custom internal tools.
- **Every day:** Engineers spent 30% of their time writing repetitive boilerplate and fixing deployment scripts.
- **One day:** Modern AI coding assistants emerged, making raw code production 10x faster.
- **Because of that:** The bottleneck shifted from typing code to review, system architecture, and quality assurance.
- **Because of that:** Without unified standards, tech debt doubled and security reviews ground to a halt.
- **Until finally:** An autonomous, rule-driven engineering platform was introduced to enforce quality at source.

## 2. Barbara Minto's Pyramid Principle (SCQA)

For executive, consulting, and stakeholder presentations, use the **SCQA** framework:

1. **Situation (S):** Objective facts everyone in the room already agrees with. Establishes alignment.
   * *Example:* "Global digital payments reached \$10T in transaction volume in 2024."
2. **Complication (C):** What changed or what threat/opportunity has arisen?
   * *Example:* "Cross-border fraud rates increased by 48%, costing merchants \$42B in chargebacks."
3. **Question (Q):** The implicit question the audience is now asking.
   * *Example:* "How can enterprise merchants verify transactions in real-time without hurting conversion?"
4. **Answer (A):** The core thesis and solution your presentation introduces.
   * *Example:* "Implement automated biometric risk scoring with instant cryptographic verification."

---

## 3. Finding the Ending First

Always write the final slide before the intermediate slides.
- What decision is being voted on?
- What budget or authorization is requested?
- What mental model shift should the audience adopt?

If you do not know the exact landing point, every preceding slide risks wandering off-topic.

---

## 4. The Unstick Moves (When Storytelling Stalls)

- **The Stakes Check:** Ask "If we do nothing for 12 months, what goes wrong?" If the answer is "not much", increase the honesty about the problem's urgency.
- **The Customer Hero:** Frame the customer as Luke Skywalker and your product/idea as Yoda. Never make your product the hero; make your product the tool that makes the customer successful.
- **Cut Half the Context:** Most decks spend 5 slides explaining background the audience already knows. Condense context into a single sharp slide and move to the tension.
